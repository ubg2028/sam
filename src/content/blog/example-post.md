For most of the last decade, "faster" was the only metric that mattered. Every product review, every engineering roadmap, every all-hands slide included a chart with response time on the y-axis and a line trending down and to the right. Speed was the goal, and anything that slowed a user down — a confirmation step, a loading state, a moment of friction — was treated as a bug to be shipped away.

That consensus is cracking. In interviews with product leads at a dozen companies over the past six months, a pattern kept surfacing: teams that removed friction indiscriminately often saw short-term engagement gains followed by longer-term trust erosion.

## The difference between friction and feedback

The teams getting this right aren't simply adding delays. They're distinguishing between friction that protects — a pause before an irreversible action — and friction that merely annoys, like an unnecessary loading spinner on a static page.

> People don't actually want instant. They want to feel like the system understood what they asked for.

## What this looks like in practice

Three patterns came up repeatedly across the teams interviewed for this piece:

- **Visible confirmation states.** A brief, clearly labeled processing step before a payment, deletion, or send action completes.
- **Explicit summaries before commitment.** Showing exactly what will happen, in plain language, before the final button.
- **Undo windows instead of instant confirmations.** A five-second window to reverse an action, with a visible countdown.

## The takeaway for smaller teams

You don't need a research team to apply this. Look at the two or three highest-stakes actions in your product and ask whether they currently happen too fast to feel considered.
